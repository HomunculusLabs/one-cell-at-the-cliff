**Kriegeskorte, Simmons, Bellgowan & Baker, "Circular analysis in systems neuroscience — the dangers of double dipping", Nat Neurosci 12:535–540 (2009), PMC2841687 — READ: abstract + introduction in full from the PMC author manuscript, plus the policy and the 34-question supplementary FAQ at the level of its own quoted text (the PMC extraction truncated the body after the introduction; DISCLOSED, the policy statements below are quoted from the paper's abstract/intro and its supplement, not reconstructed from the figures)** | The canonical statement of SELECTION BIAS in systems neuroscience: "double dipping", using the same data for selection and for selective analysis, "will give distorted descriptive statistics and invalid statistical inference whenever the results statistics are not inherently independent of the selection criteria under the null hypothesis". Three named mechanisms — selection, weighting, sorting. The remedy policy, in their order: (1) prefer a NONSELECTIVE analysis of the whole measurement volume with correction for multiple tests; (2) if selective analysis is needed, explicitly DEMONSTRATE that the results statistic is independent of the selection criterion under H0; (3) failing that, use an INDEPENDENT DATA SET for the selective analysis. They add a diagnostic we can actually run: "test your complete analysis (including all selection stages) on null data from a random generator". | The number that matters: in their simulated fMRI experiment a contrast **simulated to be exactly zero** (A−B inside a region active in both) reads **spuriously significant at p < 0.01** when the ROI is defined non-independently from the same data — even though the ROI was defined by a rigorous cluster-threshold map that correctly highlighted the truly active region — and the distortion **vanishes entirely** when an independent dataset defines the ROI. **The RATIO/INDEX prior art — Pearson, Proc R Soc 60:489–498 (1897) "On a form of spurious correlation which may arise when indices are used in the measurement of organs"; Jackson & Somers, Oecologia 86:147–151 (1991) "The spectre of 'spurious' correlations"; Forstmeier, bioRxiv 10.1101/298786 (2018) "Avoiding misinterpretation of regression lines in allometry" — READ: Pearson at abstract + results-summary level from the Royal Society scan, Jackson & Somers at abstract level (paywalled body, DISCLOSED), Forstmeier in full-text (head + tail of the bioRxiv PDF, simulation section read)** | A 129-year-old literature on exactly L224's object: a RATIO formed to "remove a size effect" does not remove it, and the correlation of an index with its own denominator has a NON-ZERO null. Pearson's result: if u and v are indices built from variables selected purely at random with no correlation among them, **correlation between u and v still appears** — he names it "spurious organic correlation" and defines it as the correlation that remains when the absolute values are distributed at random. Jackson & Somers' remedy is a RANDOMIZATION test and their stated headline is the one we need: "identifying the appropriate null correlation is of utmost importance when statistically evaluating ratios, although this issue is frequently ignored". Forstmeier's contribution is the regression-based fix and its trap. | The numbers that matter: Pearson's own worked case produces spurious index correlations of **0.4–0.5 from entirely independent variables**; Forstmeier shows that OLS regression of a trait on a size covariate **manufactures a group difference where the simulated data contain none** (males/females differing in size ONLY read as dimorphic), because biological noise in the PREDICTOR attenuates the OLS slope — in his fitted case by about **10%, slope correction factor 1/0.91 = 1.097** — and he states that accounting only for technical measurement error is NOT sufficient to remove the spurious effect. | What it changes for peabrain: (a) **L224's measured Spearman(R, m_out) = +0.53 is currently being compared against zero, and zero is the wrong null.** R = P2/(lam1^2·m_out) is an index whose denominator is the very variable it is correlated against; Pearson's result says a residual rho is EXPECTED even with no biological size dependence, and Jackson & Somers' randomisation test — recompute rho with the components shuffled, subtract — is the instrument that says how much of +0.53 is organic. Without it L224 can measure a k that zeroes a quantity whose honest null was never zero. Folded as a REQUIRED first leg below, ahead of the fit. (b) **A live correctness warning on L224 as written**: the card specifies "fit P2 ~ m_out^k (log-log OLS, and a robust Theil-Sen leg)". Forstmeier's result is that OLS with noise in the predictor ATTENUATES the slope, so the fitted k comes out too small and the residual R_k keeps a size dependence of the sign we were trying to remove — the exact failure mode the card exists to avoid, arriving through the card's own primary estimator. The fix is an errors-in-variables / reduced-major-axis (standardised major axis) leg beside OLS, with the three k estimates reported together and the spread treated as the uncertainty; Theil-Sen is robust to outliers but NOT to predictor noise and does not substitute. (c) Scope honesty: this is a biometry/ecology literature about organism measurements, not connectomics — it supplies the null-construction convention and the estimator caution, and transfers because the mathematics is about ratios, not about organs. Reference candidates for L174: Pearson 1897, Jackson & Somers 1991, Atchley/Gaskins/Anderson Syst Zool 25:137–148 (1976) on the statistical properties of ratios.
