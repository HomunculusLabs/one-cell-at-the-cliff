# Deep prior-art dig — terra, 2026-11-21

Scope: public web search and fetched abstracts/summaries, plus selected full-text passages; no experimental reruns. All numbered research items below have had their abstract or author summary fetched and read. An entry is not an assertion that every method was audited. Three entries in the DNp33 section are field-level context, not evidence that those papers tested DNp33. Access refers to what was actually retrieved; subscription previews are explicitly marked. Preprints are not peer-reviewed results. Search is not exhaustive and cannot establish global priority.

## Bottom line and strongest threats

The proposed conjunction remains distinguishable in this search, but several broad novelty formulations are already taken. Alstott's equal-size lesions invalidate any claim of being first to show location-dependent changes in connectome-model functional dynamics independent of lesion size. Habib et al. invalidate a broad claim of first identity-versus-count effect in a fly connectome motor model. Pugliese et al. substantially narrow a claim of first identified single interneuron being necessary for a motor rhythm in a connectome-scale rate simulation. The credible candidate contribution is the specific conjunction: one identified cell, shared temporal motor-pool readout, explicitly defined matched removal-mass / delivered-drive / class / sign comparators, an intervention-supported one-interneuron route, and partial locking to the external drive envelope, in the stated model. Whether all those controls and causal route tests actually succeed is a question for the paper's evidence, not resolved by this literature review.

Important correction to the existing field journal: Alstott expressly compared lesions of equal size (50 nodes); saying they had 'no mass control' without defining mass is incorrect. Equal node count is not necessarily equal removed edge weight, input drive, tissue volume, or synapse count. Likewise Bey et al. do NOT establish that mass-matched designs solve lesion-localization bias; their point is that particular compartmental regressions can mislead, even after lesion-volume correction.

## 1. C. elegans laser-ablation lineage

1. Chalfie et al. (1985), “The neural circuit for touch sensitivity in Caenorhabditis elegans,” Journal of Neuroscience. https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.05-04-00956.1985 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6565008/ . Serial EM and laser killing of cell precursors tied defined sensory/interneuron paths to touch-induced movement, including AVD/AVB and PVC paths. Foundational anatomy + targeted loss, but not motor-pool covariance, mass matching, or necessarily a single mature-cell manipulation. Free full text.

2. Rakowski et al. (2013), “Synaptic polarity of the interneuron circuit controlling C. elegans locomotion,” Frontiers in Computational Neuroscience. https://doi.org/10.3389/fncom.2013.00128 — https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computational-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fncom.2013.00128/full . Laser ablation of individual named neuron classes and combinations plus a connectome-informed small circuit model fitted forward/backward bout durations. DVA is a single unpaired neuron; other named class manipulations can remove bilateral pairs, which the model combines. Motor behavioral timing, not temporal correlation in a recorded motor pool, and no mass-matched controls. Free full text.

3. Fouad et al. (2018), “Distributed rhythm generators underlie Caenorhabditis elegans forward locomotion,” eLife. https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.29913 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5780042/ . Ablation and optogenetics reveal multiple oscillatory sections and bidirectional entrainment. Their AVB lesion generally removed both cell bodies and possibly other head cells; isolated B-neuron removal was not sufficient to abolish independent posterior rhythms. A useful negative control against overclaiming a single-cell oscillator. Free full text.

4. Gao et al. (2018), “Excitatory motor neurons are local oscillators for backward locomotion,” eLife. https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.29915 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5780044/ . Cell ablation, physiology and calcium imaging identify A-motor-neuron oscillations and state-dependent modulation by AVA via mixed electrical/chemical synapses. Sparse cell removal has local effects; distinguish classes and multiple cells from one isolated cell. Not a matched indirect-relay lesion. Free full text.

5. Chew et al. (2017), “Recordings of Caenorhabditis elegans locomotor behaviour following targeted ablation of single motorneurons,” Scientific Data. https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2017.156 — https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata2017156 . Approximately 300 videos of animals with one of five identified motor neurons ablated, including mock controls; raw curvature data enable timing analysis. Dataset paper, not evidence by itself of a significant single-cell timing phenotype. Free full text.

## 2. Stroke: location, volume, and the danger of adjustment

6. Rangaraju et al. (2015), “The role of acute lesion topography in initial ischemic stroke severity and long-term functional outcomes,” Stroke. https://doi.org/10.1161/STROKEAHA.115.009643 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4550548/ . In 490 patients, voxelwise location maps included lesion volume, age and sex as covariates; particular left-hemisphere regions remained associated with severity and outcome. Observational covariate adjustment, not matched single-neuron intervention or a proof location dominates volume for all endpoints. Free author manuscript.

7. Laredo et al. (2018), “Prognostic Significance of Infarct Size and Location: The Case of Insular Stroke,” Scientific Reports. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-27883-3 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-018-27883-3 . Two cohorts, 90 and 105 patients; volume control greatly reduced death associations while particular locations retained associations with poor functional outcome. Strong counterexample to a blanket 'location matters more than volume': mortality was mostly volume-associated. Free full text.

8. Payabvash et al. (2017), “Acute Ischemic Stroke Infarct Topology,” AJNR. https://doi.org/10.3174/ajnr.A4970 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7963653/ . In 238 cases, brainstem/thalamic lesions had higher severity-to-volume ratios; other locations tended to have both larger lesions and worse outcomes. Ratio analysis is not matching and has its own limitations. Free abstract fetched.

9. Bey et al. (2026), “Testing hypothesized neural substrates with lesions,” bioRxiv preprint, posted 19 November. https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.11.18.767704 — https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.11.18.767704v1?rss=1 . Analytic critique and semi-synthetic simulations with 4,119 ischemic lesions find regionally weighted lesion-volume regressions can show false specificity; total-volume adjustment can attenuate or exacerbate bias and reduce sensitivity. This attacks a particular class of observational localization procedures, not all lesion matching and not this model's intervention logic. Abstract fetched, preprint.

## 3. Network attacks and lesion simulations

10. Albert, Jeong & Barabási (2000), “Error and attack tolerance of complex networks,” Nature. https://doi.org/10.1038/35019019 — https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/cond-mat/0008064 . Random versus degree-targeted removal at comparable fractions of nodes; connectivity and graph diameter, not neural motor timing. Equal node fraction is not matched edge mass or delivered drive. Open author manuscript; publisher access not checked.

11. Alstott et al. (2009), “Modeling the Impact of Lesions in the Human Brain,” PLOS Computational Biology. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1000408 — https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1000408 . Dynamical cortical neural-mass connectome simulations: lesion site changes distant functional connectivity; network centrality partly predicts impact. Full text explicitly says equally sized 50-node lesions have markedly different dynamic effects. Closest historical equal-count/dynamic precedent, but regional neural masses, resting state, not individual neurons, and no demonstrated equal synaptic mass or matching of effective drive. Free full text.

12. Bellingeri et al. (2019), “The heterogeneity in link weights may decrease the robustness of real-world complex weighted networks,” Scientific Reports. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-019-47119-2 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-47119-2 . Seven weighted networks; degree/strength targeted node attack and weight-shuffled network controls expose damage invisible to binary connectivity. This explicitly handles weight but compares attack strategies, not found to match removed weighted mass across individual neuron deletions. Free full text.

13. Habib, Petr & Rust (2026), “Focal neuronal loss and cell replacement in a connectome model of fly steering,” bioRxiv preprint. https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.10.26.762043 — https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.10.26.762043v1.full . Spiking MaleCNS connectome model with motor-neuron steering readout: 4,000-cell lesions yield strongly location-dependent performance; motor-pathway-prioritized replacement of 100 cells beats random 100-cell replacement, and matched vs non-target identities change recovery. Count is matched, but identity assignment changes integration/connection load; 100-cell recovery is not single-cell ablation nor a shared temporal pool metric. This is a very close fly-model identity precedent, not evidence for an animal phenotype. Free preprint, abstract and accessible text fetched.

14. Tao & Rapp (2021), “Investigating the network consequences of focal brain lesions through comparisons of real and simulated lesions,” Scientific Reports. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-81107-9 — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-81107-9 . Chronic stroke connectomes differ from healthy connectomes with the same nodes subtracted; inferred longer-term reorganization exceeds simple subtraction. Reminder that model deletion is an acute in-silico intervention, not long-term biology. Publisher notes a correction; consult it before substantive reliance. Free abstract/full page.

## 4. CPGs, identified interneurons, and flight

15. Ritzmann, Pollack & Tobias (1982), “Flight activity mediated by intracellular stimulation of dorsal giant interneurons of the cockroach Periplaneta americana,” Journal of Comparative Physiology. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00609665 — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00609665 . Single identified giant interneuron stimulation triggers coordinated flight motor bursts, apparently polysynaptically. Strong old single-cell-to-motor-rhythm precedent; activation, not ablation; polysynaptic is not a demonstrated unique single relay. Publisher summary available; full article subscription.

16. Robertson & Pearson (1985), “Neural circuits in the flight system of the locust,” Journal of Neurophysiology. https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1985.53.1.110 — https://journals.physiology.org/doi/abs/10.1152/jn.1985.53.1.110 . Single identified interneuron pulses phase-reset flight rhythm; paired recordings support a disynaptic delayed-excitation pathway through one or more unidentified intervening neurons, and explain motor burst timing. Very close mechanistic ancestor, but stimulation, not identified-relay deletion or matched mass. Abstract fetched; full access not established.

17. Robertson (1987), “Interneurons in the flight system of the cricket Teleogryllus oceanicus,” Journal of Comparative Physiology A. https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00615077 — https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00615077 . Identified interneurons can reset flight rhythm; sensory deafferentation changes frequency and intermuscle phase latencies, with wind modulation. Deafferentation is not removal of one interneuron. Summary fetched; full article subscription.

18. Pugliese et al. (2026), “Connectome simulations identify a central pattern generator for fly walking,” Nature, published 28 October. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-026-11195-y — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11195-y ; accessible earlier author version https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13142387/ . VNC connectome rate simulations, descending activation screen, and pruning identify a three-interneuron rhythm circuit across four datasets; individual E1 or E2 silencing in the full simulation abolishes leg motor rhythms. Optogenetics tested descending-neuron predictions, not necessity of individual core CPG cells in animals. No claim here of matched synaptic-mass/drive controls, a single-relay route, or partial drive-envelope locking. Nature abstract and preprint full-text passages fetched; Nature main text subscription.

19. Seki et al. (2026), “A canonical excitatory–inhibitory motif embedded in the connectome underlies rhythmic locomotion in Drosophila larvae,” bioRxiv preprint. https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.08.31.748252 — https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.31.748252v1?rss=1 . Connectome-constrained firing-rate waves, phase recordings, and manipulations implicate A18f/A02j motif. Abstract supports cell-type-level perturbation; a search-result full-text excerpt claims effects from silencing a single A18f in some animals, but full text was rate-limited and that finer claim is not independently verified here. Preprint abstract fetched.

20. Schnell, Ros & Dickinson (2017), “A descending neuron correlated with the rapid steering maneuvers of flying Drosophila,” Current Biology. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2017.03.004 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6309624/ . AX activity correlates with rapid spontaneous and visual turns and activation appears sufficient. Not established here as DNp33, and no loss/mass matching. Free author manuscript; summary fetched.

## 5. Motor temporal structure versus rate

21. Riehle et al. (1997), “Spike synchronization and rate modulation differentially involved in motor cortical function,” Science. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.278.5345.1950 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9395398/ . Monkey M1 recordings: task-related coincidence changes, including expectancy-related synchrony without detectable firing-rate modulation. Observational, cortical spikes rather than lesioned motor pools; cannot equate a continuous rate-model correlation with spike synchrony. Abstract fetched; publisher full access not checked.

22. Grammont & Riehle (2003), “Spike synchronization and firing rate in a population of motor cortical neurons in relation to movement direction and reaction time,” Biological Cybernetics. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00422-002-0385-3 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12750898/ . Synchrony and firing rate peak at different moments and both relate to task direction. Observational, not equal-rate lesion evidence. Abstract fetched; publisher full access not checked.

23. Srivastava et al. (2017), “Motor control by precisely timed spike patterns,” PNAS. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1611734114 — https://www.pnas.org/doi/full/10.1073/pnas.1611734114 . In songbird respiratory motor units, manipulating within-pattern spike timing at fixed spike count gives behavioral and force effects. Strong causal timing-versus-count precedent, but perturbation is stimulation pattern, not a single-neuron lesion or between-neuron correlation. Abstract/significance fetched; publisher page offers purchase.

24. Dura-Bernal et al. (2016), “Restoring Behavior via Inverse Neurocontroller in a Lesioned Cortical Spiking Model Driving a Virtual Arm,” Frontiers in Neuroscience. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2016.00028 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4746359/ . Lesioned spiking cortical model, virtual reaching, and stimulation-based restoration; full-text search identifies timing/correlation analyses. Not a demonstration that single-cell deletion alters timing without rate changes. Abstract and selected page passages fetched; free full text.

## 6. DNp33 and contextual descending-neuron resources

25. Zung et al. (2025), “An updated catalogue of split-GAL4 driver lines for descending neurons in Drosophila melanogaster,” bioRxiv preprint. https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.02.22.639679 — https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.02.22.639679v1.full . Abstract verifies the expanded anatomical and driver-line catalogue, not DNp33 physiology. Indexed supplemental TSV names DNp33 driver lines, but direct TSV retrieval was blocked/unsupported; do not treat particular line specificity as verified. Preprint abstract fetched.

26. Stürner et al. (2025), “Comparative connectomics of Drosophila descending and ascending neurons,” Nature. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08925-z — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08925-z . Female brain and cord reconstructions matched across hemispheres and compared with male, including light-level driver matching; framework for homolog/type comparisons, not a verified specific functional claim for DNp33. Free article, abstract fetched.

27. Namiki et al. (2018), “The functional organization of descending sensory-motor pathways in Drosophila,” eLife. https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.34272 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6019073/ . Descending-neuron anatomical catalogue and genetic access; motor neuropil organization provides context, not by itself a DNp33 functional characterization. Free full text and abstract.

28. “Organization of circuits linking descending input to motor output in the Drosophila Male Adult Nerve Cord connectome,” eLife. https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.96084 — https://elifesciences.org/articles/96084 . MANC connectome survey finds direct DN–motor-neuron contacts infrequent and motor-system-specific communities. This supports indirect routes as a common anatomical possibility, not our particular DNp33→relay mechanism. Abstract fetched; free full page.

Direct non-paper resource: Reiser Lab Male CNS Cell Type Explorer, DNp33 page, https://reiserlab.github.io/celltype-explorer-drosophila-male-cns/types/DNp33.html (fetched). It lists anatomical input/output regions and typed partner tables, including Johnston's-organ classes and interneurons; these are structural annotations, not proof of wind coding, functional synaptic sign, or a unique causal relay. An indexed public software repository describes modeled DNp33 wind responses, but was not used as a verified citation and should not be presented as experimental physiology. No DNp33-specific functional paper or established cross-species homolog was verified in this search. Field-context entries 25–28 must not be cited as if they individually establish such a role.

## Implications for a defensible paper

- Cite Alstott with the equal-50-node detail; differentiate weighted removal mass, drive-matched controls, single-cell rather than regional scale, and motor-pool temporal structure. Do not call count matching absent.
- Cite Habib for count- and identity-dependent fly-model lesion/repair, and Pugliese for individual cell perturbation of connectome-scale rate-model motor rhythms. State whether control comparators are individual cells or types, their tolerances, number available and selection protocol.
- Specify that time-varying rates and their shared component are not millisecond spike synchronization or actual wingbeats. Report unchanged mean rates only if the actual tests support that claim. A signal partly time-locked to the input envelope is not evidence of an autonomous CPG; examine trial-shuffled/common-input and residualized measures before calling coupling intrinsic.
- Relay structure requires interventions (cut/clamp/rescue and competing paths), not just a graph path. A mechanistic statement is model-specific and limited by weight assumptions, graph version, and perturbation fragility.
- Bey's warning argues for validating the inference and reporting matching overlap, not asserting that matching automatically resolves all confounding. No new simulations or paper data were examined in this dispatch.
